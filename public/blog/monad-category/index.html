A Monad is a Monoid in the Category of Endofunctors (WIP)

Jayden Li
November 19, 2026

In progress!

I learned some category theory and abstract algebra in Math 55a at Harvard, so here’s (trying to) understand the Infamous Sentence.

Definition (Monad). A monad is a monoid in the category of endofunctors. □

Actually, this isn’t the most precise definition. I assumed “monoid” meant a monoid in abstract algebra, which it isn’t.1 We might also ask: … the category of endofunctors of what? So, we have the more precise definition of a monad below:

Definition (Monad). A monad in a category \(\newcommand{\CC}{\mathcal C} \CC\) is a monoid object in the monoidal category of endofunctors of \(\CC\). □

Now, onto defining the terms in this definition.

Categories and Functors

Definition (Category). A category \(\CC\) consists of:

  • A collection of objects \(\DeclareMathOperator{\Ob}{Ob}\Ob(\CC)\).
  • For every two objects \(A,B\in\Ob(\CC)\), a collection of structure preserving maps called morphisms \(\DeclareMathOperator{\Hom}{Hom}\Hom(A,B)\). This set of morphisms is called the hom-set. We write \(f:A\to B\) to mean a morphism from \(A\) to \(B\), and \(f\in\Hom(A,B)\).
  • A partial binary operator called composition between morphisms. For all objects \(A,B,C\in\Ob(\CC)\), a composition between morphisms: \[ \circ:\Hom(A,B)\times\Hom(B,C)\to\Hom(A,C) \] If \(f\in\Hom(A,B)\) and \(g\in\Hom(B,C)\), we have that the codomain of \(f\) is the domain of \(g\), so we have another morphism \(g\circ f\in\Hom(A,C)\). There does not necessarily exist a composition between any two morphism.

Composition must satisfy two axioms:

  • Associativity. Let \(A,B,C,D\in\Ob(\CC)\) and let \(f\in\Hom(A,B)\), \(g\in\Hom(B,C)\) and \(h\in\Hom(C,D)\). Then: \((h\circ g)\circ f=h\circ(g\circ f)\).
  • Identity. For every \(A\in\Ob(\CC)\), there exists an identity morphism \(\DeclareMathOperator{\id}{id}\id_A\in\Hom(A,A)\) such that for all objects \(B\in\Ob(\CC)\) and morphisms \(f\in\Hom(A,B)\), we have \(f\circ\id_A=f=\id_B\circ f\). □
Example: Category of finite-dimensional vector spaces

Let \(\DeclareMathOperator{\Vect}{Vect} \Vect_k\) be the category of finite-dimensional vector spaces over a field \(k\). The set of morphisms between any two objects \(V,W\in\Ob(\Vect_k)\) is the set of linear map/homomorphisms: \(\DeclareMathOperator{\LL}{\mathcal L} \Hom(V,W)=\LL(V,W)\) (using notation from Axler, LADR). \(\Hom(V,W)\) is not the set of all functions \(V\to W\) because morphisms preserve structure.

Let \(U,V,W\in\Ob(\Vect_k)\). Composition between two linear maps \(f:U\to V\), \(g:V\to W\) is simply given by standard function composition, which is associative. The identity \(\id_U\) on any vector space \(U\) is simply the map \(\id_U:u\mapsto u\) for all \(u\in U\). □

Definition (Functor). A functor is a map of categories that preserve structure. Let \(\newcommand{\DD}{\mathcal D}\CC,\DD\) be categories; a functor \(F:\CC\to \DD\) is a map satisfying the following:

  • Each object \(A\in \Ob(\CC)\) is associated to an object \(F(A)\in \Ob(\DD)\)
  • Each morphism \(f\in\Hom(X,Y), f:X\to Y\) (where \(X,Y\in\Ob(\CC)\)) is associated to a morphism \( F(f)\in\Hom(F(X),F(Y)), F(f):F(X)\to F(Y) \).

A functor \(F\) must preserve the structure of categories by preserving the identity and the composition of morphisms:

  • Recall that for each object \(A\in\Ob(\CC)\), there exists an identity morphism \(\id_A\in\Hom(A,A)\) such that \(\id_A(a)=a\) for all \(a\in A\). The identity is preserved: \(F(\id_A)=\id_{F(a)}\), the identity morphism of the object \(F(A)\in\Ob(\DD)\).
  • Let \(f\in\Hom(A,B)\) and \(g\in\Hom(B,C)\) be morphisms. By definition of the composition operator, \(g\circ f\in\Hom(A,C)\) and is also a functor. Then \(F(g\circ f)\) is a functor from \(F(A)\) to \(F(C)\). Likewise, \(F(f):\Hom(F(A),F(B))\) and \(F(g):\Hom(F(B),F(C))\), so their composition \(F(g)\circ F(f)\) is a functor from \(F(A)\) to \(F(C)\). Functors preserve the composition of morphisms, so we require \(F(g\circ f)=F(g)\circ F(f)\). □

Definition (Endofunctor). An endofunctor is a functor from a category \(\CC\) to itself. \(\DeclareMathOperator{\End}{End}\End(\CC)\) is the collection2 of all endofunctors of \(\CC\): \(\End(C)=\{F:\CC \to \CC\}\). □

Two claims: that \(\End(\CC)\) can itself be given a structure of not just a category, but of what’s called a monoidal category, so \(\End(\CC)\) is the “monoidal category of endofunctors of \(C\).” First, we define a monoidal category.

Monoidal Categories

Definition (Product category). Let \(\CC,\DD\) be categories. The product category \(\CC\times\DD\) is similar to the notion of a Cartesian product of sets.

  • The objects of \(\CC\times\DD\) are pairs of objects \((A,B)\) with \(A\in\Ob(\CC)\) and \(B\in\Ob(\DD)\), i.e. \(\Ob(\CC\times\DD)=\Ob(\CC)\times\Ob(\DD)\), where the \(\times\) on the right hand side is the Cartesian product of sets.
  • A morphism from \((A,B)\in \Ob(\CC\times\DD)\) to \((A',B')\in \Ob(\CC\times\DD)\) is a pair, consisting of a morphism in \(\CC\) from \(A\) to \(A'\) and a morphism in \(\DD\) from \(B\) to \(B'\). Therefore, the hom-set in the product category is: \(\Hom_{\CC\times\DD}((A,B),(A',B')) = \Hom_{\CC}(A,A') \times \Hom_{\DD}(B,B')\); again, the right hand side uses the Cartesian product.
  • The composition of two morphisms \((f,g)\) and \((f',g')\) is given by pairwise composition of morphisms in categories \(\CC\) and \(\DD\): \((f,g)\circ(f',g')=(f\circ_\CC f', g\circ_\DD g')\).
  • The identity morphism associated with the object \((A,B)\in\Ob(\CC\times\DD)\) is the pair \(\id_A,\id_B\), which are the identity morphisms in the respective categories \(\CC\) and \(\DD\).

In short: the objects of \(\CC\times\DD\) is the Cartesian product of the objects of both categories, and morphisms are defined naturally as pairs.□

Definition (Monoidal category). A monoidal category is a category \(\CC\) together with the following to be defined:

  • A bifunctor \(\otimes:\CC\times\CC\to\CC\) (a functor whose domain is a product category) called the tensor product.
  • A unit object \(I\in\Ob(\CC)\).
  • “Associativity”: let \(X,Y,Z\in\Ob(\CC)\). By definition of the tensor product, \((X\otimes Y)\otimes Z\) and \(X\otimes(Y\otimes Z)\) are both objects in \(\CC\). The requirement here is that they are isomorphic. More specifically, to define a monoidal category, we must define a natural isomorphism (invertible morphism in \(\CC\)) between these for all \(X,Y,Z\). This isomorphism is called the associator: \(\alpha_{X,Y,Z}\) is an isomorphism such that \(\alpha_{X,Y,Z}:(X\otimes Y)\otimes Z \xrightarrow{\sim} X\otimes(Y\otimes Z)\).
  • For each object \(X\in\Ob(\CC)\), we must define a left unitor \(L_X\) and a right unitor \(R_X\), which are natural isomorphisms: \(L_X:I\otimes X\xrightarrow{\sim} X\) and \(R_X:X\otimes I\xrightarrow{\sim} X\).

And also, the associator and unitor natural maps need to make the following diagrams commute below. For all \(W,X,Y,Z\in\Ob(\CC)\):

\[\begin{CD} ((W\otimes X)\otimes Y)\otimes Z @>{\alpha_{W\otimes X,Y,Z}}>> (W\otimes X)\otimes(Y\otimes Z) \\ @V{\alpha_{W,X,Y}\otimes\mathrm{id}_Z}VV @VV{\alpha_{W,X,Y\otimes Z}}V \\ (W\otimes(X\otimes Y))\otimes Z @. W\otimes(X\otimes(Y\otimes Z)) \\ @V{\alpha_{W,X\otimes Y,Z}}VV @| \\ W\otimes((X\otimes Y)\otimes Z) @>>{\mathrm{id}_W\otimes\alpha_{X,Y,Z}}> W\otimes(X\otimes(Y\otimes Z)) \end{CD}\]

This is called the pentagon axiom, and it says that the two ways of reparenthesizing \(((W\otimes X)\otimes Y)\otimes Y\) result in the same object in \(W\otimes(X\otimes (Y\otimes Z))\). The other constraint is called the triangle axiom:

\[ \begin{CD} (X\otimes I)\otimes Y @>{\alpha_{X,I,Y}}>> X\otimes(I\otimes Y) \\ @V{R_X\otimes\mathrm{id}_Y}VV @VV{\mathrm{id}_X\otimes L_Y}V \\ X\otimes Y @= X\otimes Y \end{CD} \]

Similiarly, this says that you can eliminate the unit object \(I\) directly with the unitor, or you can reparenthesize \((X\otimes I)\otimes Y\) with the associator and then eliminate the identity. Both must give the same outcome.□

Note that some of the properties above are about “natural isomorphisms.” They are not equal! For example, the category of sets, with the bifunctor being the Cartesian product, is a monoidal category.3 If \(X,Y,Z\) are sets, \((X\times Y)\times Z\) contain elements of the form \(((x,y), z)\), but \(X\times(Y\times Z)\) contains \((x,(y,z))\). They are not equal. But, there is a natural (and very obvious) isomorphism \((X\times Y)\times Z\xrightarrow{\sim} X\times (Y\times Z)\): the associator would be \(\alpha_{X,Y,Z}: ((x,y),z) \mapsto (x,(y,z)) \). But, since we are interested in structure, being isomorphic means they have the same structure and the two collections are “the same” up to renaming the elements, so they are good as equal.

Ok, this is quite a complicated definition. It may be helpful to look at some examples below.

Returning to the set of endofunctors of a category, it turns out that \(\End(\CC)\) can be given the structure of a monoidal category. We introduce a definition that is useful to show this.

Natural Transformations

Definition (Natural transformation). A natural transformation is a morphism between two functors. Let \(\CC,\DD\) be categories, and let \(F,G:\CC\to\DD\) be functors. A natural transformation \(\alpha:F\Rightarrow G\)4 is the assignment from every object \(x\in\Ob(\CC)\) to a morphism \(\alpha_x:F(x)\to G(x)\) (\(\alpha_x\) is called the component of \(\alpha\) at the object \(x\)) in \(\DD\) (since \(F,G\) are functors, \(F(x),G(x)\in\Ob(\DD)\)), such that for all objects \(x,y\in\Ob(\CC)\) and morphisms \(\phi:x\to y\) in \(\CC\), the following diagram commutes:

\[ \begin{CD} F(x) @> F(\phi) >> F(y) \\ @V \alpha_x VV @VV \alpha_y V \\ G(x) @>> G(\phi) > G(y) \end{CD} \]

Note: \(F(x),F(y),G(x),G(y)\in\Ob(\DD)\), and \(\alpha_x,\alpha_y,F(\phi),G(\phi)\) are morphisms in \(\DD\).

The diagram above commutes if and only if the following morphisms in \(\DD\) are equal: \(G(\phi)\circ \alpha_x\) and \(\alpha_y \circ F(\phi)\), and \(\circ\) is composition defined in \(\DD\). To put it simply, we require that \(\alpha\) makes the diagram commute in order for it to preserve the structure of categories \(\CC,\DD\) and that of its functors/morphisms. □

Example: Vector space to its double dual

Let \(V\) be a vector space over a field \(k\). Recall its dual space \(V^*\) is:

\[ V^*=\{\text{linear maps } V\to k\}=\LL(V,k) \]

The double dual \(V^{**}\) (also written \((V^*)^*\)) of \(V\) is the dual space of \(V^*\):

\[ V^{**}=\{\text{linear maps } V^*\to k\}=\LL(V^*,k) \]

\(V\) is naturally isomorphic to \(V^{**}\). Why?

We can do so by the evaluation map. We associate any \(v\in V\) to a map \(V^*\to k\) as such: for any \(\ell:V\to k, \ell\in V^*\), we map it to its value evaluated at \(v\) by \(\ell\mapsto \ell(v)\), which gives a map from \(V^*\) to \(k\). Intuitively, this map is natural because we do not need to “choose” anything. It is also injective and surjective. Thus \(V\) is naturally isomorphic to \(V^{**}\).

In category theoretical terms: \(V,V^{**}\in\Ob(\Vect_k)\) (since the linear maps form a vector space). We have the identity functor \(\id:\Vect_k\to\Vect_k\) that maps each vector space \(V\) to itself, and maps each morphism/linear map \(T:V\to W\) to itself.

The double dual is also a functor \(\Phi\). It maps objects/vector space \(V\) to \(V^{**}\), obviously. If \(V,W\in\Ob(\Vect_k)\), then morphisms are linear maps \(T:V\to W\). \(\Phi\) should map \(T\) to a linear map in the double dual spaces \(V^{**}\to W^{**}\). We can do so by the transpose of linear maps.5 The first transpose of \(T\) is \(^tT:W^*\to V^*\), and the second transpose is \(^t\left(^t T\right):V^{**}\to W^{**}\).

Specifically, if \(V,W\in\Ob(\Vect_k)\) and \(T:V\to W\) is a linear map, then the double dual functor \(\Phi\) does the following:

\[ \Phi(V)=V^{**} \qquad \Phi(T)={} ^t\left(^t T\right) \]

\(\Phi(T)={}^t\left(^tT\right)\) is not literally the same thing as \(T\), of course, as they are linear maps between different vector spaces. But, we have a natural association between them.

This satisfies the functor laws. Briefly, if \(\id_V\) is the identity morphism/linear map in \(V\), then \(\Phi(\id_V)=^t\left(^t \id_V\right)=\id_{V^{**}}\) by definition of the transpose. Likewise, for composition:

\[ \Phi(S\circ T) ={}^t\left(^t(S\circ T)\right) ={}^t \left(^tT\circ {}^tS\right) ={}^tS \circ {} ^tT \]

So \(\Phi\) preserves both composition and the identity, so \(\Phi\) is indeed a functor.

We can define the natural transformation \(\alpha\) between functors \(\id\) and \(\Phi\) as such: for each object/vector space \(V\in\Ob(\Vect_k)\), define the component \(\alpha_V:\id(V)\to\Phi(V)\) (i.e. \(V\to V^{**}\)) as follows, for \(v\in V\):

\[ \alpha_V(v)=(\underbrace{\ell}_{V^*} \mapsto \underbrace{\ell(v)}_{k}) \in V^{**} \]

i.e. the evaluation map with \(\ell:V\to k\) as any arbitrary element of \(V^*\). We do not need to make a choice for \(\ell\). We need to check: \(\alpha_V\) is indeed a morphism/linear map in \(V^{**}\), and it makes the diagram in the definition commute.

By definition, each component \(\alpha_V\) must be a morphism in \(V^{**}\), i.e. a linear map. Indeed \(\alpha_V\in V^{**}\), so it remains to check whether it is linear. Let \(v,v'\in V\): \begin{align*} \alpha_V(v+v’) &=(\ell\mapsto \ell(v+v’)) =(\ell\mapsto (\ell(v)+\ell(v’))) \\ &=(\ell\mapsto\ell(v))+(\ell\mapsto\ell(v’)) =\alpha_V(v)+\alpha_V(v’) \end{align*} Since \(\ell\in V^*\) is linear. The sum of elements in the dual space is defined as the pointwise sum, and scalar multiples are defined as pointwise scalar multiples. Let \(\lambda\in k\) be a scalar value: \begin{align*} \alpha_V(\lambda v) &=(\ell\mapsto \ell(\lambda v)) =(\ell\mapsto (\lambda\ell(v))) \\ &=\lambda(\ell\mapsto\ell(v)) =\lambda\alpha_V(v) \end{align*} So \(\alpha_V\) is indeed linear, so it is a morphism in \(V^{**}\).

Since we claim to have a natural transformation \(\id\Rightarrow\Phi\) we want the following diagram to commute (since \(\Phi(V)=V^{**}\) and likewise for \(W\)):

\[ \begin{CD} V @> T >> W \\ @V \alpha_{V} VV @VV \alpha_W V \\ V^{**} @>> \Phi(T) > W^{**} \end{CD} \]

where \(V,W\in\Ob(\Vect_k)\) are objects/vector spaces, \(T:V\to W\) is a morphism/linear map, and \(\Phi\) is the double dual functor defined previously. Recall \(\Phi(T)={}^t\left(^tT\right):V^{**}\to W^{**}\), the double transpose of \(T\).

We check that \(\Phi(T)\circ \alpha_V=\alpha_W\circ T\). Clearly, both are maps \(V\to W^{**}\). Let \(v\in V\).

\[ (\alpha_W\circ T)(v) =\alpha_W(T(v)) =(\ell\mapsto \ell(T(v))) \]

Note that unlike before, \(\ell\in W^*\) (instead of \(V^*\)). The above is indeed defined because \(T(v)\in W\), so \(\ell(T(v))\in k\).

Note \((\Phi(T)\circ\alpha_V)(v)=\left(^t\left(^t T\right)\right)(\alpha_V(v))\). By definition, \(^t\left(^t T\right):V^{**}\to W^{**}\) and is given by \(\left(^t\left(^t T\right)\right)(\mu)=\mu\circ{}^t T\) with \(\mu\in V^{**}\) (this follows by taking the transpose of \(^tT\)). Since \(\alpha_V(v)\in V^{**}\):6

\[ (\Phi(T)\circ\alpha_V)(v) =\left(^t\left(^t T\right)\right)(\alpha_V(v)) ={} \alpha_V(v)\circ{}^tT \]

Check types: \(\Phi(T)\circ\alpha_V:V\to W^{**}\), so we expect the value above (which is the a map \(V\to W^{**}\) evaluated at \(v\in V\)) to be an element of \(W^{**}\), i.e. a map \(W^*\to k\). The input would be an input to \(^tT:W^*\to V^*\), which is \(W^*\) as expected. The output is the output of \(\alpha_V(v):V\to k\), which is \(k\). OK.

We know the composition above is an element of \(W^{**}\), or a map \(W^*\to k\). Interpret this as “given an element \(\ell\in W^*\), we first use \(^tT\) to map it to an element of \(V^*\), then evaluate that at \(v\in V\) by \(\alpha_V\).” To do this, we can write:

\[ (\Phi(T)\circ\alpha_V)(v) =(\ell \mapsto \left(\left(^tT\right)(\ell)\right)(v) ) \]

Again, by definition of the transpose \(^tT:W^*\to V^*\), we have \(\left(^tT\right)(\ell)=\ell\circ T\in V^*\).

\[ (\Phi(T)\circ\alpha_V)(v) =(\ell \mapsto (\ell\circ T)(v) ) =(\ell \mapsto \ell(T(v)) ) \]

This is exactly \((\alpha_W\circ T)(v)\) from before, so we conclude that the diagram does indeed commute by our definitions of \(\alpha\) as the evaluation map and \(\Phi\) as the double transpose. Thus, we have demonstrated above a natural transformation \(\alpha:\id \Rightarrow \Phi\).

Last thing: notice that \(\id\) and \(\Phi\) are both endofunctors: \(\id\) obviously, but \(\Phi\) takes vector spaces \(V\) to double dual vector spaces \(V^{**}\), so in fact \(\Phi:\Vect_k\to\Vect_k\). As we’ll see next we can give \(\End(\Vect_k)\) the structure of a category such that \(\alpha_V\) is a morphism between objects \(\id\) and \(\Phi\).

Now we have enough for the proof that \(\End(\CC)\) is a monoidal category.

Monoidal Category of Endofunctors

Proof (\(\End(\CC)\) is a monoidal category). The objects of \(\End(\CC)\) are endofunctors \(F:\CC\to\CC\). The morphisms are a bit more complex. What actually is a morphism? In familiar categories like the category of sets or \(\Vect_k\), morphisms are functions/linear maps between two sets/vector spaces. These would be natural ways to define a morphism in these particular categories. But morphisms of categories in general do not have to be maps. In general, the hom-set of two objects of a category is just a “thing that is provided when you define a category \(\CC\),” and the hom-sets collectively have some additional properties like identity and composition. Morphisms are just “things” in the hom-set; what they specifically are depends on the category.

For clarity, we will use \(\Hom_X\) to denote a hom-set in the category \(X\), since we will be working with \(\CC\) as well as the (supposed) category \(\End(\CC)\).

Let \(F,G\in\Ob(\End(\CC))\), so \(F,G:\CC\to\CC\) are endofunctors of \(\CC\). We define the morphisms as follows: the hom-set \(\Hom_{\End(\CC)}(F,G)\) is the set of natural transformations of functors \(F\Rightarrow G\).

Example: Other objects and endofunctors of \(\End(\Vect_k)\)

Previously, we defined the identity \(\id\) and double dual \(\Phi\) endofunctors on \(\Vect_k\). If \(V\) is a vector space over a field \(k\) and \(V^{**}\) is its double dual, we defined a natural transformation \(\alpha:\id\Rightarrow\Phi\) with components \(\alpha_V(v)=(\ell \mapsto \ell(v))\) where \(\ell\in V^*\).

But this is not the only natural transformation we can choose. For example:

\[ \alpha_{V,\lambda}(v)=(\ell \mapsto \ell(\lambda v)) \]

where \(\lambda\in k\) is a scalar. This will still make the diagram commute as part of the definition of a natural transformation. Actually, these (scalar multiples as above) are the only natural transformations from the identity to the double dual functor.

Since \(\id,\Phi\) are endofunctors of \(\Vect_k\), the “scalar multiple evaluation maps” above are morphisms (elements of the hom-set) in \(\End(\Vect_k)\), but they are not the only ones. For example, we could have the direct sum and tensor product endofunctors (\(S\) and \(P\), respectively): \begin{alignat*}{2} S(V)&=V\oplus V &\qquad S(T)&=T\oplus T \\ P(V)&=V\otimes V &\qquad P(T)&=T\otimes T \end{alignat*} where \(T:V\to W\) is a linear map. \(S\) and \(P\) are additional objects in \(\End(\Vect_k)\). We have some other natural transformations such as \(\id\Rightarrow S,S\Rightarrow S,P\Rightarrow P\), among others, which are in turn morphisms in \(\End(\Vect_k)\).

Two things to check: the structure above makes \(\End(\CC)\) a category and a monoidal category.

\(\End(\CC)\) is a Category

By definition of a category, we must have a composition operator satisfies the associativity and identity axioms. Let \(F,G,H\in\Ob(\End(\CC))\) be objects/endomorphisms. Recall that composition is the following partial operator:

\[ \circ:\Hom(F,G)\times\Hom(G,H)\to\Hom(F,H) \]

Let \(\alpha\in\Hom(F,G)\) and \(\beta\in\Hom(G,H)\) be morphisms of \(\End(\CC)\), i.e. natural transformations \(\alpha:F\Rightarrow G, \beta:G\Rightarrow H\). For all \(X,Y\in\Ob(\CC)\) and morphisms \(\phi:X\to Y\) in \(\CC\), we have the following commutative diagrams by definition of natural transformations:

\[ \begin{CD} F(X) @> F(\phi) >> F(Y) \\ @V \alpha_X VV @VV \alpha_Y V \\ G(X) @>> G(\phi) > G(Y) \end{CD} \qquad \begin{CD} G(X) @> G(\phi) >> G(Y) \\ @V \beta_X VV @VV \beta_Y V \\ H(X) @>> H(\phi) > H(Y) \end{CD} \]

Now we stack these diagrams as such, since they share the same line \(G(X) \xrightarrow{G(\phi)} G(Y)\). When we write \((\beta\circ\alpha)_X=\beta_X\circ\alpha_X\), by \(\circ\) in the right hand side we mean “composition of morphisms of the category \(\CC\).” Note that the components \(\beta_X,\alpha_X\) associate an object \(X\in\Ob(\CC)\) to a morphism in \(\CC\), so composing \(\beta_X\) and \(\alpha_Y\) uses composition defined in \(\CC\). We write \(*\) for composition in \(\CC\).

\[ \begin{CD} F(X) @> F(\phi) >> F(Y) \\ @V \alpha_X VV @VV \alpha_Y V \\ G(X) @> G(\phi) >> G(Y) \\ @V \beta_X VV @VV \beta_Y V \\ H(X) @>> H(\phi) > H(Y) \end{CD} \implies \phantom{\beta_X} \begin{CD} F(X) @> F(\phi) >> F(Y) \\ @V \beta_X * \alpha_X VV @VV \beta_Y * \alpha_Y V \\ H(X) @>> H(\phi) > H(Y) \end{CD} \]

Composition gives a natural transformation \(\beta\circ\alpha:F\Rightarrow H\), which we define as: for each object \(X\in\Ob(\CC)\), define the component of \(\beta\circ\alpha\) at \(X\) as \((\beta\circ\alpha)_X=\beta_X * \alpha_X\) (composition in \(\CC\)). Then, the diagram above becomes:

\[ \begin{CD} F(X) @> F(\phi) >> F(Y) \\ @V (\beta\circ\alpha)_X VV @VV (\beta\circ\alpha)_Y V \\ H(X) @>> H(\phi) > H(Y) \end{CD} \]

Which is exactly the naturality square satisfied by a natural transformation \(\beta\circ\alpha\), so we have a valid definition for the composition of morphisms/natural transformations in \(\End(\CC)\).

To check that composition in \(\End(\CC)\) is associative, let \(F,G,H,K\in\Ob(\End(\CC))\) be endofunctors, and let \(\alpha:F\Rightarrow G, \beta:G\Rightarrow H, \gamma:H\Rightarrow K\) be natural transformations. For all objects \(X\in\Ob(\CC)\), the component at \(X\) is: \begin{align*} ((\alpha\circ\beta)\circ\gamma)_X &=(\alpha\circ\beta)_X * \gamma_X =(\alpha_X * \beta_X) * \gamma_X \\ (\alpha\circ(\beta\circ\gamma))_X &=\alpha_X * (\beta_X \circ \gamma_X) =\alpha_X * (\beta_X * \gamma_X) \end{align*} Since composition \(*\) in \(\CC\) is associative by definition, the above lines are equal, and composition \(\circ\) in \(\End(\CC)\) is also associative.

The other composition axiom to check is the existence of an identity. Specifically, for all \(F\in\Ob(\End(\CC))\), we need an identity morphism/natural transformation \(\id_F:F\Rightarrow F\), such that for all \(G\in\Ob(\End(\CC))\) and natural transformations \(\alpha:F\Rightarrow G\), we have \(\alpha\circ \id_F = \alpha=\id_G\circ \alpha\). This is accomplished simply by defining the components of \(\id_F\) at \(X\in\Ob(\CC)\) as \((\id_F)_X=\id_{F(X)}:F(X) \to F(X)\), the identity morphism X) in \(\CC\) which must exist by definition of the category \(\CC\). This choice of \(\id_F:F\Rightarrow F\) satisfies the identity axiom (proof omitted).

To summarize, we’ve defined composition of natural transformations \(\alpha,\beta\) as \((\alpha\circ\beta)_X=\alpha_X * \beta_X\), and found that it satisfies:

  1. \(\alpha\circ\beta:F\Rightarrow H\) is also a natural transformation.
  2. It is associative.
  3. There exists an identity morphism, namely the identity natural transformation.

Thus, we conclude that \(\End(\CC)\) with objects endomorphisms and morphisms natural transformations indeed forms a category.

\(\End(\CC)\) is a Monoidal Category

Define tensor product \(\otimes\)

Let \(F,G,F',G'\in\Ob(\End(\CC))\) be endofunctors of \(\CC\), and \(\alpha:F\Rightarrow F', \beta:G\Rightarrow G'\) are morphisms/natural transformations of functors. Define the tensor product/bifunctor \(\otimes\) on objects as \(F\otimes G=F\circ G\) (composition, as defined previously). By definition of a bifunctor, we want \(\alpha\otimes\beta: F\otimes G \Rightarrow F'\otimes G'\), which by definition of \(\otimes\) on objects is \(\alpha\otimes\beta:F\circ G \Rightarrow F'\circ G'\). In other words, we want the component of the natural transformation \(\alpha\otimes\beta\) at \(X\) to be a morphism \((F\circ G)(X)\to (F'\circ G')(X)\). Consider the following diagram:

\[ \begin{CD} F(G(X)) @> F(\beta_X) >> F(G'(X)) \\ @V \alpha_{G(X)} VV @VV \alpha_{G'(X)} V \\ F'(G(X)) @>> F'(\beta_X) > F'(G'(X)) \end{CD} \]

I claim this diagram commutes. Why? View \(\beta_X\) as a morphism: by definition of the natural transformation \(\beta\), its component \(\beta_X\) is a morphism \(\beta_X:G(X)\to G'(X)\). To see this more clearly, match each of the following symbols (in quotation marks) in the definition of a natural transformation to the following symbols here:

  • “\(\phi\)” to \(\beta_X\).
  • “\(x\)” to \(G(X)\), and therefore “\(\alpha_x\)” to \(\alpha_{G(X)}\).
  • “\(y\)” to \(G'(X)\), and therefore “\(\alpha_y\)” to \(\alpha_{G'(X)}\).
  • “\(F\)” to \(F\) and “\(G\)” to \(F'\).

And the diagram above easily follows from the fact that \(\alpha\) is a natural transformation. This tells us that we can define \(\alpha\otimes\beta\) in two equivalent ways:

\[ (\alpha\otimes\beta)_X =\alpha_{G'(X)} \circ F(\beta_X) =F'(\beta_X) \circ \alpha_{G(X)} \]

We choose to use the first one. Now we verify that \(\alpha\otimes\beta\) is in fact a natural transformation \(F\circ G \Rightarrow F'\circ G'\).

  1. In abstract algebra, a monoid is a set \(S\) together with a binary operator \(m:S\times S\to S\) called the law of composition satisfying two axioms (using the shorthand for the law of composition \(ab:=m(a,b)\) for all \(a,b\in S\)):

    • Identity. There exists an element \(e\in S\) such that \(ae=ea=a\) for all \(a\in S\).
    • Associativity. For all \(a,b,c\in S\), we have \((ab)c=a(bc)\).

    (If a monoid satisfies the inverse axiom, then it is also a group: for all \(a\in S\), there exists an inverse \(b\in S\) such that \(ab=ba=e\).) As we’ll see, the a monoid object in category theory is a generalization of a monoid defined above. ↩︎

  2. \(\End(C)\) is not always be a set, because it might be too big. For example, consider the category \(\CC\) of all sets. We have an embedding of all sets (i.e. objects of \(\CC\)) to an endofunctor of \(\CC\) as such: for all \(A\in\Ob(\CC)\), let \(K_A:\CC\to\CC\) be a mapping that maps all objects of \(\CC\) to the set/object \(A\), and all morphisms to \(\id_A\). \(K_A\) satisfies the requirements of a functor (preservation of composition and identity), and is thus an endofunctor. There is an associated endofunctor \(K_X\in\End(\CC)\) for each object in \(\CC\), so we have an embedding of every set in \(\End(C)\). But, a set cannot contain all sets (or have an embedding of all sets), so \(\End(C)\) is not a set. In this case, \(\End(C)\) is in fact a proper class. For “small” categories \(\CC\), however, \(\End(\CC)\) is actually a set. ↩︎
